## I. The Harbour

The traveller woke to the sound of wings.

They lay among grasses growing through the foundations of a road, beneath a sky that seemed ordinary until they looked at it too long. Beyond the hillside, trees swallowed the ruins of buildings whose purposes had been forgotten. A few standing stones shimmered in the morning light. Through one of them, for the space of a heartbeat, the traveller thought they saw the road whole again.

They could remember nothing before waking. Not a home, a name, or why the world sometimes appeared to have two answers to the same question.

A small bird stood nearby. It was exhausted, its feathers dulled, yet whenever the traveller moved, it followed. When something unseen shifted beyond the trees, the bird looked up before the sound came.

A wild creature charged from the brush. The traveller barely had time to raise a weapon. Then the bird cried out, and for an instant the creature seemed to move twice: once as a pale suggestion, and once in the flesh. The traveller stepped aside from an attack that had not quite happened yet.

The glimpse vanished. The bird nearly fell from the air.

Later, the people of an inland village told the traveller that the world had always been strange. There were places where a house could be seen standing and ruined at once, where a path led to a cliff one day and disappeared the next. Their ancestors had come inland after the destruction of a city called Harbour. They spoke of a fleet of invaders, a beast from the sea, and great birds that had once protected the coast.

Some told the story as a warning. Others had stopped believing it.

When creatures attacked the village, the traveller fought alongside its defenders. The small bird, recovering its strength, began to do more than warn: for a moment it could hold a charging animal in place, long enough for frightened villagers to escape. The people did not agree on what they had witnessed, but they agreed the stranger had helped them.

The traveller asked about Harbour. No one encouraged the journey. The old coastal approach had become impassable, and the ruins offered nothing but danger.

The traveller went anyway.

In a narrow valley above the sea, a waterfall plunged across the remains of a path. The cliff had been eaten away; there was no safe way around. But the bird circled a patch of trembling air beside an older stone channel. Beyond the shimmer was the same valley, green and unfamiliar, with the water running along another course.

For the first time, the bird found the strength to widen a seam.

The traveller stepped through and stood in the Past.

The old diversion was still intact, though its stones had shifted and its course could be changed. The traveller worked at it until the water began flowing away from the cliff. At first nothing seemed to happen beyond the noise of the stream.

Then the seam flashed.

Through its surface the traveller saw the Present waterfall shudder, dissolve, and reappear farther down the slope. The ruined path re-formed as a narrower, dry passage. The seam itself trembled and became impassable. The bird landed beside it, gathered itself, and opened the way again.

The traveller crossed back into a future that had never existed a few moments earlier.

At Harbour, the cliffs opened onto a landscape shaped by something far greater than water.

A colossal skeleton lay among the ruins. Ribs rose above collapsed streets like the beams of a second, impossible city. The skull faced the sea. Whatever had once come ashore here had been terrible enough to destroy Harbour, but it had not survived.

And through a seam among the broken walls, Harbour still lived.

The traveller entered a busy settlement preparing for an attack. Watchers on the headland had spotted ships approaching from the east. The defenders called them invaders. Along the quays, people argued over one of the last great birds. It had been confined partly for its own protection and partly because its warnings of rough seas and approaching danger were too valuable to lose. Those who kept it believed they were safeguarding Harbour; others argued that protection had become captivity. Few understood that the bird sensed something deeper than weather or ordinary threats.

The traveller recognised the fleet's desperation before anyone else did. These were refugees, not conquerors. They had crossed a dangerous sea to escape the collapse of their own homeland. The defenders had prepared for the wrong enemy.

The traveller worked to stop the two communities from killing one another and freed a captured great bird. But the real threat was already gathering beyond the harbour wall.

The water split.

A creature from a time older than either civilisation rose through a rupture in the sea. It towered over ships and battlements, enormous and bewildered, dragged into an age where it did not belong. It would destroy Harbour without ever knowing what Harbour was.

The battle was fought by people who had almost become enemies. The traveller warned defenders of attacks moments before they came, held great limbs still for precious seconds, and—with the small bird's growing power—dragged the creature briefly back along a movement it had just made. Each opening let the defenders move civilians, protect the quays or strike from safer ground. The rescued great bird returned at the moment it was needed, but it could not win the battle for them.

Together they brought the creature down before Harbour fell.

When the traveller passed back through the seam, the skeleton was gone.

Harbour had grown rather than died. Streets climbed the hillsides; ships filled the bay; the descendants of refugees and coastal families lived together. Traces of the inland village's people remained in names, customs and stories, but the community the traveller remembered had never developed in quite the same way. Great birds wheeled over the sea, no longer reduced to legend.

The traveller remembered both cities: the dead one and the living one. No one else did.

And across the water, in the direction the refugees had come from, lay the reason Harbour had been endangered in the first place.

## II. Across the Sea

A great bird carried the traveller east.

The farther they travelled, the less the world seemed able to agree with itself. In abandoned streets, doors opened into buildings that were no longer there. Hillsides displayed the scars of roads that had never been cut. People remembered floods that had left no watermarks and harvests from fields that had never existed.

Yet through the seams, the Past of this land was dazzling.

The Concordance had built cities of bridges, gardens, workshops and extraordinary machines. Its greatest achievement was the Loom: a network of artificial anchors that held temporal passages open long after natural seams would have settled.

There were few signs of the causally attuned birds in these cities. The people spoke of them as creatures from an earlier age, absent for generations. What looked like extinction had begun more quietly: as artificial passages remained open, the birds' natural ranges became increasingly disturbed, and they withdrew from the places where people once knew them. Some populations had dwindled; others might have survived beyond the network's reach. Through these openings people could physically travel into earlier moments, move supplies and intervene in events whose consequences would echo into their own lives.

At first, the Loom had been merciful. A famine prevented. A sickness contained before it spread. A war diverted before armies met.

Then its successes became a habit.

A failed harvest no longer meant adapting the farms; it meant finding a year in which it could have gone differently. A shortage meant opening another passage and carrying supplies out of the Past. The work was pushed farther from the thriving heart of the Concordance, toward places whose inhabitants would bear consequences the cities might never see.

The Curator believed this was the price of keeping a civilisation alive. They were neither a monster nor a fool. The traveller saw why people trusted them.

Scattered among the installations were traces of an older ambition. The first anchors had been built by people who studied the natural seams and the birds that sensed them, hoping to prevent suffering without forcing every passage to remain open. The Curator and another researcher had helped turn that hope into machinery. Their work had saved lives; only gradually had they disagreed about how often the passages should be held open, and whether a civilisation could learn to endure what it could not correct. The traveller did not yet know why that forgotten disagreement mattered to them.

At one damaged installation, the traveller helped its workers contain a dangerous failure. Lives were saved because the Loom could still be made to function. The relief on the workers' faces was real. So was their gratitude.

Across other regions, the traveller found smaller natural seams that the bird noticed without any machinery. Some led to lost places and modest discoveries. Others revealed that the prosperity of one settlement had depended on a change made somewhere distant, to people who had not agreed to pay for it.

The problem was not merely what the Loom did through its gateways. It was that the gateways had been forced to remain open, and that every attempted correction could create another contradiction demanding its own correction. The world had been rewritten too often, pressed too hard to continue existing in a single shape.

At a neglected anchor, the failure was unmistakable.

The machine held open a rip reaching far beyond ordinary history. Smaller prehistoric creatures were spilling into a settlement, attacking people who had relied on that same gateway for essential deliveries. The traveller helped repel the animals and reached the installation. Shutting it down closed the opening. The creatures already inside remained, but no more came through.

The people had been saved from one danger and exposed to another.

Without the anchor, the deliveries stopped. Some thanked the traveller for ending the attacks. Others asked how they were supposed to survive the coming months. The Curator argued that the failure proved the need for better maintenance, not destruction of the system.

The traveller had no easy answer.

But across the damaged Present, there was evidence the Curator could not see—or could not bear to accept. The Loom was not keeping disaster away indefinitely. It was delaying it, and spreading its cost.

By the end of the journey, repairing another broken installation no longer felt like a solution. It felt like making the last one stay open.

The traveller chose to dismantle the network.

## III. The Bird-Rider

The work began without spectacle.

Some anchors could be reached by exploring a ruined district or crossing a natural seam into the Past. Some required navigating old machinery or persuading people to step aside. Others brought a fight. Each closure ended one forced temporal connection; it did not undo the historical choices already made through it.

Across the Concordance, however, life had been built around those connections.

Goods and travellers were rerouted through the remaining anchors. Operators drove those machines harder. People whose jobs, food and families depended on the network watched its passages close, one after another, and saw the traveller as the cause.

They began calling the stranger the Bird-Rider.

At one great settlement, the strain became impossible to ignore. Its remaining anchor was carrying traffic and demands it had never been meant to bear. Streets shook. Essential routes failed. The people had to leave.

The traveller helped defend the evacuation, guiding frightened families through damaged streets and keeping attackers away from the withdrawing crowds. There were people who owed their lives to the Bird-Rider. There were also people who looked back at their homes and saw the person whose actions had made the crisis immediate.

Both things were true.

Among those fleeing west were the people the traveller had encountered at Harbour, in another version of the Past. The same desperate journey was beginning again, but this time there was a chance to keep it from becoming a massacre.

The final installation stood near the great marine fault. Beyond it, the sea was restless with an instability deeper than the Loom's other wounds. The Curator and the defenders still believed the network might be saved. Closing its heart seemed to them like deliberately surrendering everything they had built.

The traveller knew what the open fault would one day allow into this world.

The great creature had already destroyed Harbour once. The traveller had fought it and changed that history. But if the main gateway remained, the disaster still had a place to begin.

As refugees departed, the traveller and the birds helped hold the crossing long enough for them to pass. Then the traveller reached the mechanism at the heart of the fault and shut it down.

There was no final battle with the sea creature.

No towering shape rose from the water. No skull came to rest among Harbour's streets. The terrible thing had been prevented so completely that, in the new history, it had never happened.

To the people watching from the shore, the Bird-Rider had destroyed the Concordance's last great machine while their civilisation was already breaking apart.

The Curator had tried to preserve their world. The Bird-Rider had ended it.

Neither account was entirely false.

The traveller's own existence had begun with another failed intervention, one made in the final desperate days of the history that was now being erased. They had once been sent into time alongside a newly discovered bird, intended to save a world already falling apart. The experiment had missed its destination by centuries and taken their memories with it.

Now the traveller stood apart from the history they had changed. Whatever price that severance would finally demand, there could be no simple return to the life they had lost.

Centuries later, Harbour was still there.

There was no skeleton among its buildings, no great battle marked in its oldest records. The descendants of western families and eastern refugees shared the coast. Great birds crossed the sky above them, their lineage unbroken.

In the surviving histories of the east, the Bird-Rider was remembered as the destroyer of the old civilisation. The Curator appeared in some accounts as its final guardian. Elsewhere, in smaller stories passed through families who had escaped, there were uncertain memories of a stranger with a bird who had shown them the way out.

The traveller could remember a Harbour of ruins, a Harbour saved by battle, and a Harbour that had never needed saving from the sea.

The world remembered only the last of them.

**History cannot record the disasters that didn't happen.**

This story is a **readable adaptation**, not a new authority. In particular:

- **Locked underlying narrative:** one overwritable chronology; original exile and amnesia; two accessible eras; inland village; historical river change; Harbour's refugees, giant prehistoric creature and rescued great bird; prosperous Present v2; Concordance/Loom and Curator; containment and neglected-anchor crises; anchor shutdown cascade and evacuation; prevention of the marine catastrophe; contested Bird-Rider legacy.
- **Direction rather than canon:** precise scenery, dialogue-free story staging, the relative attention given to particular missions, and the family accounts in the final Present.
- **Agreed character direction:** the protagonist and Curator helped build the anchor system together, then gradually diverged over bird-like limited intervention versus increasingly continuous correction. The protagonist does not yet know their role in that shared history. Exact relationship, roles and timing are open.
- **Still open:** the exact sacrifice tied to causal severance; when/how the original experiment is revealed in the playable game; the protagonist's eventual ability to return or remain after the final intervention; exact NPC identities and companion relationships; individual anchor mechanics and geographical layouts.
